# The island with no pollen

In June 1925 Werner Heisenberg, 23 and miserable with hay fever, took leave from Göttingen for Helgoland, a rocky island in the North Sea with almost no vegetation to torment him.

He had decided to stop describing electron orbits, since nobody had ever seen one, and to build a theory from measurable things: the frequencies and brightnesses of the light atoms emit. That forced him into arrays of numbers, one entry per pair of energy levels. Combining two arrays, he found the answer depended on the order of multiplication.

He was convinced he had made an error, then convinced he had not. He wrote later that he was too excited to sleep and climbed a rock at the island's southern tip to wait for sunrise.

Back in Göttingen, Max Born recognised the arrays: matrices, with the non-commutation you know from linear algebra. Within a year Schrödinger had a rival theory built from waves and differential equations, the two were proved equivalent, and Born added the rule that the squared amplitude is a probability.

Three years, one revolution, a century of arguing about what it means.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why did Heisenberg end up with non-commuting quantities?
A: He built the theory only from observable transition data between energy levels, so each quantity became a two-index array — a matrix. Matrix multiplication is order-dependent, and in this case the order-dependence was physical: it is exactly the relation $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ that later turns into the uncertainty principle.
