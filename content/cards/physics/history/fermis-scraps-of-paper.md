# Fermi's scraps of paper

At 5:29 in the morning of 16 July 1945, in the desert of southern New Mexico, the Manhattan Project tested its plutonium device. Most of the physicists lay face down behind an earth barrier with welding glass over their eyes.

Enrico Fermi did something else. He had torn a sheet of paper into small pieces. About forty seconds after the flash, as the blast wave reached him, he let them fall from about six feet and watched where they landed — roughly two and a half metres downwind. He had worked out beforehand how the displacement relates to the energy released, and announced on the spot that the yield was around ten thousand tons of TNT.

The instrumented analysis took weeks. It came in higher — modern re-analysis puts the yield near 25 kilotons — so Fermi was low by a factor of two and a half, using confetti.

There had been a betting pool on the yield. Teller said 45 kilotons, Bethe 8, Oppenheimer 0.3; Ramsey bet on a complete dud. Rabi arrived late, found 18 kilotons still available, and won.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What makes Fermi's paper-scrap estimate a good piece of physics rather than a party trick?
A: It is dimensional analysis under field conditions: the blast displacement depends on the energy released, the distance and the air density, so measuring one number he could see gave him the one he could not. Being within a factor of about two on an unknown of this magnitude, forty seconds after the event, is exactly what a Fermi estimate is for.
