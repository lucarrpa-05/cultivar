# They blamed the pigeons

In 1964 Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson had a beautiful horn antenna at Bell Labs in Holmdel, New Jersey, and a problem. Their receiver picked up a faint hiss that would not go away. It came from every direction of the sky, day and night, in every season — ruling out the Sun, the galaxy, New York City and every known radio source.

They spent months eliminating possibilities. They checked the electronics. They found pigeons nesting in the horn, evicted them, and scrubbed out what Penzias delicately called a white dielectric material. The hiss stayed: an excess antenna temperature of a few kelvin that no instrument fault could explain.

Thirty miles away at Princeton, Robert Dicke's group was building a detector to look for exactly this — the cooled afterglow of a hot early universe, predicted decades earlier and never seriously sought. A phone call, a visit, and the two groups published back-to-back in 1965: Princeton explaining what it meant, Bell Labs reporting a measurement, under a title that mentions only an excess antenna temperature.

They took the 1978 Nobel Prize for noise they had spent a year trying to remove.

Q: What is the cosmic microwave background, physically?
A: The light released when the universe cooled enough for electrons and nuclei to combine into neutral atoms, about 380,000 years after the Big Bang, so that photons could travel freely for the first time. It was a glow at roughly 3,000 K then; 13.8 billion years of expansion have stretched its wavelengths by a factor of about 1,100, cooling it to 2.725 K — microwaves, arriving from every direction at once.
