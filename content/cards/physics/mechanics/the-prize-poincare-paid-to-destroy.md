# The prize Poincaré paid to destroy

Two bodies orbiting each other is solved: ellipses, forever, since Newton. Add a third and the equations stop being solvable in the old sense. King Oscar II of Sweden turned this into a competition for his sixtieth birthday, and in January 1889 Poincaré won: 2,500 kronor and a gold medal.

The memoir went into *Acta Mathematica*. While preparing it, the editor Edvard Phragmén kept pressing Poincaré on one delicate point. Poincaré looked again and found a real error: he had assumed a stability that was not there. The issue was already printed, and copies had gone out.

Mittag-Leffler quietly chased them down. Poincaré paid for the reprinting himself — more than 3,500 kronor, about a thousand more than the prize. Two copies survive at the Institut Mittag-Leffler; one is inscribed "The whole edition was destroyed."

The corrected 1890 memoir is the one that matters. Fixing the error forced him to see that orbits could tangle so intricately that, he wrote, he would not even attempt to draw the figure. That is the first description of deterministic chaos. The expensive mistake was the discovery.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What did Poincaré's error, once fixed, actually reveal about the three-body problem?
A: That bounded, perfectly deterministic orbits need not be stable or periodic. Near a homoclinic intersection the trajectories weave into an infinitely fine tangle, so arbitrarily small differences in the starting point grow into completely different futures. Deterministic does not mean predictable.
