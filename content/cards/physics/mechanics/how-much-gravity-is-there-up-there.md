# How much gravity is there where astronauts float?

The International Space Station orbits about 400 km up. Earth's radius is 6,371 km, so the station sits roughly 6% further from the centre of the Earth than you do. Gravity falls off as one over distance squared.

Before you read on: what fraction of surface gravity is acting on an astronaut on the station? And if the answer is what the arithmetic says, why is she floating?

Newton got there first, with a thought experiment about a cannon on a very tall mountain. Fire the ball slowly and it lands. Fire it faster and it lands further away. Fire it fast enough and the ground curves away from underneath it exactly as fast as the ball falls, and it never lands at all. It is still falling the entire time.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What fraction of Earth's surface gravity acts on an astronaut aboard the ISS, and why does she float?
A: About 89%. $g = 9.81 \times (6371/6771)^2 \approx 8.7\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$. She floats because she and the station are both in free fall around the Earth at the same rate — weightlessness is falling together, not the absence of gravity. Orbit is just falling sideways fast enough to keep missing the ground.
