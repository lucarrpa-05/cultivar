# The power you can measure is the weakest kind

Robert Dahl gave political science a clean definition in 1957: A has power over B to the extent that A can get B to do something B would otherwise not do. It is testable. Find a decision, find who wanted what, see who won. Dahl studied New Haven this way and concluded that no single elite ran the city.

Bachrach and Baratz replied in 1962 that he had looked only where the light was. There is a second face: deciding what gets decided. A committee that never puts a question on the agenda never loses a vote about it. The conflict is invisible because it is pre-emptied.

Steven Lukes added a third in 1974, the uncomfortable one. Power also shapes what people want. If a group never forms the grievance — accepts the arrangement as natural, or as its own fault — no conflict is observed at all. Lukes's test: compare what people want with what they would want under relative autonomy.

The problem, which Lukes concedes, is that the third face is nearly impossible to measure. The most consequential kind of power is precisely the kind that leaves no data behind.
