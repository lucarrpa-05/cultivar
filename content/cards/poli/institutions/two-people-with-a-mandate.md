# Two branches, both elected, no referee

Two offices, one claim: *the people chose me*. Juan Linz built a short, alarming argument on that in the first issue of the *Journal of Democracy*, 1990: presidential systems carry a structural fault that parliamentary ones do not.

Start with dual legitimacy. The president was elected by the whole country; so was the congress. When they disagree, both can truthfully say the people chose them, and the constitution offers no democratic tiebreaker. In a parliamentary system the question cannot arise, because the executive exists only while it keeps a majority.

Then add rigidity. A fixed term is a promise that a failed president stays for four more years and a successful one leaves anyway. Parliaments can replace a prime minister on a Tuesday afternoon. Linz's third point is the winner-take-all logic: one indivisible prize makes elections zero-sum, which rewards outsiders who campaign against the whole political class.

Scott Mainwaring and Matthew Shugart answer that Linz generalised from Latin America's coups and ignored how much depended on party systems. The United States has run a presidential system for two centuries. Deadlock, though, is not a bug anyone has fixed.
