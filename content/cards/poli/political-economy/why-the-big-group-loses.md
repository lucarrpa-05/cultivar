# Why the group with more members usually loses

A tariff on sugar costs each consumer a few dollars a year and hands each of a dozen producers millions. Consumers outnumber producers a hundred thousand to one and lose every time. Mancur Olson's 1965 book says that is not a scandal, it is arithmetic.

If a benefit reaches everyone in a group whether or not they helped, each member asks a private question: does my share of the gain exceed my cost of acting? In a small group one member's share can be big enough that they act alone and let the rest ride free. In a huge group nobody's share is worth the effort, so the group stays *latent* — a real shared interest with no organisation behind it. Olson's fix is the selective incentive: a private benefit only for participants. Unions offer legal insurance. Professional bodies sell the licence.

Then Elinor Ostrom spent decades finding groups that manage shared resources anyway — Swiss alpine pastures, Spanish irrigation huertas, Japanese common forests, Philippine zanjera canals — some running for centuries. *Governing the Commons* (1990) pulled eight design principles out of the survivors, and she won the Nobel in 2009.

## Rigor

Take a pure public good and a group of $n$ identical members. Each unit provided costs $c$ and yields gross group benefit $V$, shared equally, so each member gets $V/n$ per unit.

- **Efficient** provision requires $V > c$.
- **Individually rational** provision requires $V/n > c$.

The wedge is the factor $n$. As $n$ grows the second condition fails long before the first. That is Olson's claim that large groups are less able to act on a common interest, made precise — with the sharper point that inequality helps: if one member's share $F_i V$ alone exceeds $c$, that member supplies the good and everyone else free-rides. Olson calls such a group *privileged*; between it and the latent group he puts the *intermediate* case, small enough that one member's abstention gets noticed.

Ostrom's reply is that the one-shot payoff table is the wrong model. Her cases are repeated games among people who observe each other, talk, and punish cheaply. Her principles read like conditions for sustaining a cooperative equilibrium with real institutions: clear boundaries, rules fitted to local conditions, users writing their own rules, monitors who *are* the users or answer to them, graduated sanctions, cheap conflict resolution, the right to organise left unchallenged from outside, and, for a commons sitting inside a larger system, nested layers. Monitoring by the appropriators is the cheap part, and cheapness is why it lasts.
