# An impossibility theorem is a list of things you can give up

Arrow did not prove democracy impossible. He proved four properties are jointly unsatisfiable, and a theorem like that is really a menu.

Give up unrestricted domain and the trouble often evaporates. If preferences are *single-peaked* — each person has a favourite point and likes things less the further you move from it — Duncan Black showed in 1948 that the median voter's ideal point beats everything else pairwise. No cycle. That is why left-right politics is usually stable and three-cornered arguments are not.

Give up ordinal rankings, which is Amartya Sen's line: Arrow's rules may only know who ranks what above what. Let a rule use intensities and the impossibility softens into a design problem.

Give up fixed preferences. Fishkin, List, Luskin and McLean measured real deliberative polls in 2013 and found people's preferences moved measurably closer to single-peaked after discussion. Deliberation does not aggregate preferences; it reshapes them.

Which is how the German Democratic Republic could keep the word. Democracy was never one mechanism. It is a family of answers to "who decides, and how would we know", and Arrow proved the family will never contain a perfect member.
