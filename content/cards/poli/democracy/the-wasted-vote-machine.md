# The rule that manufactures two parties

Maurice Duverger noticed in 1951 something close to a law of nature: countries that elect one representative per district, by simple plurality, end up with two big parties. Countries with proportional representation end up with many. The electorate's opinions are not the variable. The counting rule is.

Two separate mechanisms do it. The **mechanical** one is arithmetic: a party with 15% spread evenly across every district wins 15% of the votes and zero seats, because it comes second or third everywhere. The **psychological** one is voters reacting to that arithmetic: once you know your favourite cannot win the seat, voting for them feels like throwing the vote away, so you vote for the least-bad survivor. The first effect punishes small parties; the second makes voters do the punishing in advance.

The caveats matter. Canada, India and the United Kingdom all run plurality elections and all have more than two parties, because a party concentrated in one region wins seats there on few national votes. William Riker, reviewing the evidence in 1982, argued the law survives as a tendency, and that the regional exceptions are the tell.
