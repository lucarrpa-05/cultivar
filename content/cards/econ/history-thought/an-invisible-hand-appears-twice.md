# An invisible hand, twice, and never "the"

Adam Smith used the phrase twice in the two books he published: once in *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759), about a landlord whose spending feeds the people who work for him, and once in *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), in a passage arguing that merchants prefer to invest at home.

Both times he wrote "an invisible hand", never "the invisible hand", and never as the name of a principle. The capital-letter doctrine was assembled by twentieth-century economists, Samuelson prominently among them.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What is Smith actually arguing in the Wealth of Nations passage?
A: Something narrow: that merchants, from a preference for investing near home, end up supporting domestic industry without intending to. It is one example of unintended consequences, not a general theorem that self-interest produces the best outcome — the general claim came much later, and the theorem version is the first welfare theorem.
