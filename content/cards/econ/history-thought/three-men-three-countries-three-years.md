# Three men, three countries, one idea, three years

Classical economics could not explain why water is nearly free and diamonds are not. Water is essential; diamonds are decoration. If value came from usefulness, the prices should be reversed — and if it came from labour, as Smith and Ricardo held, you had to explain why so much labour goes into something so useless.

In 1871 William Stanley Jevons published *The Theory of Political Economy* in England, and Carl Menger published his *Grundsätze* in Vienna. Neither knew of the other. Léon Walras, in Lausanne, published his *Éléments* in 1874. All three said the same thing: stop asking what a good is worth in general, and ask what *one more unit* is worth to someone who already has some.

You have plenty of water, so the next litre does almost nothing. You have no diamonds, so the first one does a great deal. Value is about the margin, and the margin is a derivative — which is how economics became a subject with calculus in it.

Hermann Heinrich Gossen had published the same insight in 1854, to an audience of almost nobody. Jevons found out later and admitted the priority in print.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: How does marginal thinking dissolve the diamond–water paradox?
A: By separating total usefulness from the value of one more unit. Water's total usefulness is enormous, but it is abundant, so the *marginal* litre is worth almost nothing — and price is set at the margin, not by the total.
