# The bees had contracts

James Meade built the standard classroom example of an externality in 1952: the apple grower and the beekeeper. Bees pollinate the orchard, blossoms feed the bees, and neither is paid for what they give the other. Nobody can charge a bee. So both do too little, and only a subsidy can fix it. It went into the textbooks.

In 1973 Steven Cheung went to Washington State and read the contracts. Beekeepers and farmers had been trading these services for years, in writing, with prices that moved sensibly: farmers *paid* rental fees for hives on crops that needed pollination, while beekeepers paid the farmer for access to crops that yielded good nectar. There were customary rates, seasonal terms, and enforcement by reputation in a small industry.

The lesson Coase had already argued in 1960: an externality is not a property of a good, it is a symptom of the cost of making a deal. Where the parties are few, identifiable, and dealing repeatedly, the deal gets made.

The honest limit is right there in the conditions. Nobody has yet found the contract between a coal plant and everyone downwind.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What does the bee case actually show about externalities?
A: Not that they are imaginary — that whether one persists depends on transaction costs. Few parties, clear property rights and repeat dealing make private bargains possible; millions of diffuse victims and no assignable rights make them impossible, which is where taxes and regulation earn their place.
