# Three journals rejected the paper that won the Nobel

George Akerlof wrote it in the late 1960s, a short piece about used cars. The *American Economic Review* turned it down as trivial. The *Review of Economic Studies* said the same. The *Journal of Political Economy* rejected it on the grounds that it could not be correct, since if it were, no goods could be traded at all. The *Quarterly Journal of Economics* published it in 1970, and it became one of the most cited papers in economics. Akerlof shared the 2001 Nobel for it.

The argument fits in a paragraph. Sellers know whether their car is a lemon; buyers do not. Buyers can only offer a price reflecting average quality. At that price, owners of good cars decline to sell — their car is worth more than the average. So the cars that remain are worse, the average falls, the price falls, and more good cars withdraw. The market can unravel until only lemons are left.

Which is why so much machinery exists just to make quality visible: warranties, certifications, brands, inspections, a shop's reputation. Every one is an expensive workaround for a missing piece of information.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why does the market for good used cars unravel rather than just clearing at a lower price?
A: Because the price buyers offer depends on average quality, and the sellers who withdraw at that price are exactly the ones above average. Withdrawal lowers the average, which lowers the price, which triggers more withdrawal — the price and the quality chase each other down.
