# Tulip mania is mostly a story about pamphlets

Every account of a bubble starts in Holland in 1637: a whole society trading bulbs at the price of houses, then ruin, bankruptcy, bodies in the canals. It is the origin myth of financial folly, and Charles Mackay put it into permanent circulation in 1841.

Anne Goldgar went into the Dutch archives and found a much smaller event. Trading was concentrated among a few hundred merchants and well-off craftsmen who already knew each other. Prices for rare bulbs did reach extraordinary levels — the *Semper Augustus* most of all — but she could identify fewer than half a dozen people in financial trouble afterwards, and no suicides.

The crash itself was largely a refusal to honour contracts that had never been paid for: most deals were promises to deliver in summer, made in winter, with no money changing hands. When prices collapsed, buyers simply walked away, and the courts declined to enforce.

Mackay's sources were satirical pamphlets written by moralists at the time, copied by later authors with enthusiasm. The bubble that taught everyone about bubbles was, mostly, a story people enjoyed telling.

type: reveal
Q: What is the honest version of tulip mania?
A: A real but narrow speculative episode among a small circle of Dutch merchants, in which prices for rare bulbs peaked in February 1637 and collapsed. The mass ruin, the suicides and the nation gone mad come from contemporary satirical pamphlets, relayed by Mackay in 1841 and repeated ever since.
