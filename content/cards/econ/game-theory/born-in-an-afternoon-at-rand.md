# The prisoner's dilemma was invented to embarrass Nash

In 1950 two mathematicians at the RAND Corporation in Santa Monica, Merrill Flood and Melvin Dresher, wrote down a small two-player game. They were not looking for a parable about trust. They were testing whether the equilibrium concept John Nash had just published described what people would actually do.

They ran it a hundred times with two colleagues — the economist Armen Alchian and the mathematician John Williams — and kept the players' running commentary. The players cooperated far more often than the equilibrium allowed, and complained bitterly at each other when they did not. RAND worked on nuclear strategy; nobody there thought this was an academic curiosity.

It was Albert Tucker who gave the matrix the story that made it famous: two prisoners, separate cells, a prosecutor offering each one a deal. Not a single payoff changed. The name did, and that is why you have heard of it.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What did Flood and Dresher's hundred rounds actually show?
A: That two well-informed players cooperate far more often than the equilibrium predicts, and resent each other when they do not. The game was built as a test of Nash equilibrium as a description of behaviour, and it failed that test on day one.
