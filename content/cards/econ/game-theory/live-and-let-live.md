# The trenches invented cooperation without meaning to

By 1915 stretches of the Western Front had settled into something high command detested. British and German units facing each other across a few hundred metres had quietly stopped trying to kill one another. Rations came up at predictable hours and nobody shelled them. Artillery hit the same patch of ground at the same time each day, so everyone knew where to stand. Snipers missed on purpose.

The historian Tony Ashworth documented the system; Robert Axelrod used it in 1984 as the best real-world case of cooperation emerging between enemies with no communication, no trust, and every order to shoot.

The mechanism is repetition. The same two units faced each other for months, so restraint today bought restraint tomorrow. Headquarters broke it the only way it could: rotating units and ordering raids that forced casualties, destroying the single ingredient cooperation needed.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Which ingredient did headquarters destroy, and why did that work?
A: The shadow of the future. Cooperation in a repeated game depends on expecting to meet the same opponent again; a unit that will be moved next week, and is ordered to inflict casualties this week, has no future relationship left to protect.
