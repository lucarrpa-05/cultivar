# I am auctioning a dollar, and the runner-up pays too

The rules. I auction a one-dollar bill in five-cent steps. Highest bidder wins the dollar and pays their bid. Here is the twist: the *second*-highest bidder also pays their bid, and gets nothing at all.

Bidding five cents for a dollar is obviously worth it. So is ten. Now suppose the bidding has reached 90 cents and someone bids 95. You are about to lose 90 cents for nothing — and bidding one dollar turns that into breaking even. So you bid a dollar. Now *they* are about to lose 95 cents for nothing, and bidding 1.05 turns a 95-cent loss into a 5-cent loss.

Martin Shubik published this in 1971 and ran it on students for years. Where does it stop — and what exactly was the mistake?

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Where does the bidding stop, and which bid was the error?
A: It does not stop on its own: from any position, one more bid beats conceding, so the escalation stays locally rational far past a dollar. The error is the *first* bid, which commits you to a contest where the loser pays. Shubik's target was escalation in general — sunk costs plus a penalty for coming second is the structure of price wars, patent races and wars.
