# The card dealers did not flinch, and that is the interesting part

The mug result is famous because it is so easy to run: hand out a mug, open a market, watch almost nothing trade. John List went to sports-card conventions and ran the same design on people who buy and sell small collectibles all weekend, every weekend.

Novices behaved like the Cornell undergraduates. Experienced dealers did not. The more market experience a subject had, the closer their behaviour came to the textbook prediction — and List's follow-up experiment showed the experience was doing the work, rather than traders simply being unusual people to begin with.

Then Plott and Zeiler pushed harder in 2005: run the elicitation with full training, anonymity and practice rounds, they argued, and the gap between what sellers demand and what buyers offer can disappear entirely. That claim is contested — other groups get the gap back with careful procedures — and it is the most honest thing to say about the endowment effect today: robust in the laboratory, smaller in markets, and still argued about at the level of experimental plumbing.
