# Fifteen societies, one game, and no two played it alike

The ultimatum game is two moves long. I propose a split of about a day's wages; you accept and we both get paid, or you refuse and we both get nothing. Self-interest says take anything above zero. Western undergraduates famously do not: they offer 40–45% and reject lowballs out of something that looks a lot like spite.

In the late 1990s a team of anthropologists and economists carried the game to fifteen small-scale societies — Amazonian horticulturalists, Tanzanian foragers, Mongolian herders, Indonesian whale hunters. Group mean offers ran from 26% to 58%. Not one group matched the self-interested prediction, and none of them looked much like the students either.

The Au and Gnau of Papua New Guinea rejected *generous* offers about as readily as stingy ones, in a culture where accepting a large gift creates an obligation you may not want. The Lamalera, who cannot land a whale alone, made the highest offers in the sample.

Two things predicted a group's generosity: how far it was integrated into markets, and how much everyday life rewarded cooperation.
