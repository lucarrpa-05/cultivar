# A nudge is real, and about six times smaller than you read

Two numbers from the same literature. Stefano DellaVigna and Elizabeth Linos went through 126 randomised trials run by two of the largest US nudge units — 23 million participants, every trial those units ran, whether or not anyone wrote it up. Average effect on take-up: 1.4 percentage points, an 8.1% relative improvement.

Then they looked at the academic papers testing the same kinds of intervention. Average effect: 8.7 percentage points, a 33.5% improvement. Six times bigger.

The gap is not that governments are clumsy. The authors show that publication bias, amplified by small samples, accounts for the *whole* difference: an underpowered study only clears significance when its estimate happens to be large, and those are the ones that reach print. The pattern repeated in 2022 when a PNAS meta-analysis reported a healthy average nudge effect and a reply in the same journal, correcting for publication bias, found essentially nothing left of it.

So nudges work, cheaply, at a scale worth having. And every headline effect size you have read is too big. Both sentences.
