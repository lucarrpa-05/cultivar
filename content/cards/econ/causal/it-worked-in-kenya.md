# It worked in Kenya. Would it work in Boyacá?

Miguel and Kremer randomised deworming pills across seventy-five schools in western Kenya in the late 1990s. School attendance rose substantially, including in *untreated* schools nearby, because fewer worms in circulation means fewer infections for everyone. Per year of extra schooling it was one of the cheapest interventions anyone had measured, and the design is about as clean as field economics gets.

Now a ministry in Bogotá asks whether to buy the pills for schools in Boyacá.

Before you answer, write down the list: what exactly has to be true about Boyacá for the Kenyan number to be the right prediction? Try for four items, and be specific about which ones you could check with data you can actually get.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What has to hold for the Kenyan estimate to transport?
A: (1) The mechanism must be present: the effect runs through infection, so worm prevalence has to be comparable, and in highland Colombia it mostly is not. (2) The counterfactual must match — if children are already treated through the health system, the gap you are buying is smaller. (3) Scale matters: much of the benefit came from externalities, so a partial rollout behaves differently from a universal one. (4) Implementation is part of the treatment. And the honest caveat: the deworming literature went through a public reanalysis fight, and the schooling effects are contested.
