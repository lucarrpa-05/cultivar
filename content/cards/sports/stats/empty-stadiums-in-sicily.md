# The referees changed, and nobody had told them anything

On 2 February 2007 a police officer, Filippo Raciti, was killed during rioting at the Sicilian derby between Catania and Palermo. Italian football stopped. When it restarted, clubs whose grounds did not meet safety standards were ordered to play their home matches behind closed doors.

Two Swedish economists, Per Pettersson-Lidbom and Mikael Priks, saw what nobody else did: a natural experiment. Same league, same teams, same squads, same referees — and, for a handful of fixtures, no crowd. Everything that usually gets blamed for home advantage (travel, pitch familiarity, the players' nerves) was held fixed. Only the spectators vanished.

The refereeing changed. With the stands empty, the tilt in fouls and cards toward the home side shrank sharply. Their 2010 paper in *Economics Letters* puts it plainly: referees "exhibited home bias caused by social pressure when spectators were present."

Which reframes home advantage entirely. Part of it was never about the home team at all. It was about twenty thousand people leaning on one person in the middle who was trying very hard to be fair.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What made the empty Italian stadiums of 2007 a *natural experiment* rather than just an anecdote?
A: The closures were imposed for reasons unrelated to any team's quality or form — stadium safety compliance — so the presence of a crowd was as good as randomly assigned across matches. Everything else about home advantage stayed fixed, leaving crowd pressure as the only thing that moved.
