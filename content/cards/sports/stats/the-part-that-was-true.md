# The 2002 Oakland A's were not smarter. They were shopping in the right aisle.

Oakland won 103 games in 2002 and reeled off 20 straight between 13 August and 4 September, on a player payroll of about \$44 million. The Yankees passed \$125 million that season. Michael Lewis turned it into *Moneyball* in 2003, and the world filed it under "nerds beat scouts".

That is not the claim. Oakland's edge was a *price* claim: the labour market for baseball players was paying a lot for batting average and stolen bases and almost nothing for on-base percentage, even though getting on base is what stops innings from ending. Billy Beane was not buying good players. He was buying an underpriced input.

And the tell that it was a price story, not a wisdom story, is what happened next. Hakes and Sauer ran the regressions in the *Journal of Economic Perspectives* in 2006: on-base percentage really was underpaid before the book, and within about two seasons after it, the discount was gone. The market read the book and repriced.

Which is the uncomfortable half. Every Moneyball edge destroys itself by being explained.

Q: What is the best evidence that Oakland's advantage was a market mispricing rather than superior judgement?
- [ ] They won 103 games in 2002 — a good season is consistent with skill, luck or mispricing; it does not distinguish them.
- [x] The pay premium for on-base percentage jumped within two years of the book's publication — a mispricing is something that can be arbitraged away once it is public, and it was.
- [ ] Other clubs hired statisticians — adoption shows the idea spread, but not that the original edge was about price.
