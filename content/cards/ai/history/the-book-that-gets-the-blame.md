# The book everyone blames for the first neural-network winter

Marvin Minsky and Frank Rosenblatt were at the Bronx High School of Science together, a year apart. They spent the 1960s arguing in public about whether learning machines were a serious idea. In 1969 Minsky and Seymour Papert published *Perceptrons*. In July 1971, on his 43rd birthday, Rosenblatt drowned in a boating accident on Chesapeake Bay.

The standard story — the book killed the field for fifteen years — is a legend. Funding for connectionism did collapse, but military money was already rotating away from it, and the work never stopped: Amari, Grossberg, Fukushima and Werbos all published through the supposed dead years.

What is documented is the reception. Jordan Pollack's verdict is that a narrow proof about a global issue was read as a successful attempt to bury the whole idea. The famous word is theirs, and it is from 1969, not from the backprop era: they called the extension to many-layered machines *sterile* and invited the field to reject that judgement. Nobody did for seventeen years, and the 1988 expanded edition reprinted the text with a fresh prologue and epilogue — no retraction.
