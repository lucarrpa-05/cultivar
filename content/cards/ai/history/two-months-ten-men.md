# Two months, ten men, and the word that stuck

A whole research field is named in one sentence of a funding request. Dated 31 August 1955, signed by John McCarthy, Marvin Minsky, Nathaniel Rochester and Claude Shannon:

> We propose that a 2 month, 10 man study of artificial intelligence be carried out during the summer of 1956 at Dartmouth College in Hanover, New Hampshire.

The working hypothesis is the next line: that every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can in principle be described so precisely that a machine can simulate it.

McCarthy later wrote that he coined "artificial intelligence" partly to get out from under "cybernetics" — which would have meant accepting Norbert Wiener as the field's guru, or else arguing with him.

The workshop itself was a mess. People arrived and left on their own schedules, nobody agreed on an agenda, and the summer produced no shared theory. What it did produce was a name, a mailing list, and the conviction that this was one subject rather than six. Those turn out to be the hard parts.
