# Nobody in 1967 was asking about a lever

Philippa Foot's 1967 paper is called "The Problem of Abortion and the Doctrine of the Double Effect", it appeared in the *Oxford Review*, and the runaway tram is one example among several. Her case is not the one you know: you are the **driver** of a tram whose brakes have failed, and you can steer onto one of two tracks, five workers on one and one on the other. What interested her was the old distinction between harm you intend and harm you merely foresee.

Judith Jarvis Thomson moved the reader out of the cab. In "Killing, Letting Die, and the Trolley Problem" (*The Monist*, 1976) and "The Trolley Problem" (*Yale Law Journal*, 1985), the agent becomes a bystander at a switch, and she added the footbridge: a large man whose body would stop the trolley. Same arithmetic, five for one, and almost everyone's intuition flips.

Then the part that never gets told. In "Turning the Trolley" (2008) Thomson changed her mind and argued the bystander may *not* turn it. The case's own inventor stopped accepting the standard answer.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: How does Foot's original trolley case differ from the one everyone cites?
A: In Foot (1967) the agent is the tram's driver, already steering a lethal vehicle, and the example serves a paper on abortion and the doctrine of double effect. Thomson (1976, 1985) introduced the bystander at the switch and the footbridge variant — and in 2008 argued the bystander should not turn it.
