# Three pages in 1963, and the definition collapsed

Knowledge is justified true belief. Believe it, be right, and have good reason — that was the working definition for a very long time. In 1963 Edmund Gettier, a young lecturer who had published nothing, sent three pages to the journal *Analysis*. Two counterexamples, no theory, no fix offered.

Here is the first. Smith has excellent evidence that Jones will get the job, and has counted ten coins in Jones's pocket. So he believes, with good reason, that the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket. In fact Smith gets the job — and, unknown to him, Smith has ten coins in his own pocket.

His belief is true. It is justified. And calling it knowledge feels absurd, because it came out true by accident, through a route that had nothing to do with why he believed it.

What Gettier exposed is that ordinary justification does not exclude luck. Sixty years of repairs have followed — no false premises, reliable processes, safety, sensitivity, defeasibility — and Linda Zagzebski argued in 1994 that any account combining truth with fallible justification will regenerate the problem.
