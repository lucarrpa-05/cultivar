# The man who memorised the whole rulebook

John Searle, 1980, in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*. You are locked in a room. Chinese characters come in under the door. An enormous English rulebook tells you which characters to send back out. You follow it perfectly, and outside, fluent Chinese speakers conclude they are having a conversation.

You do not understand a word of Chinese. And, says Searle, neither does any computer running any program, however good the conversation, because a program is exactly that rulebook: syntax all the way down, with no meaning attached anywhere.

The strongest objection is the **systems reply**: of course *you* do not understand Chinese, any more than a neuron understands English. The understanding, if any, belongs to the whole system — person plus rulebook plus paper.

Searle's counter is to eliminate the system. Memorise the rulebook. Do the lookups in your head. Walk out of the room. Now you *are* the entire system, and you still do not understand a word.

Whether that works is one of the longest arguments in the field. Many think it assumes the conclusion: that if understanding were there, whoever runs the computation would notice.
