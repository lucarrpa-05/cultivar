# If you are so clever, why aren't you rich?

Aristotle tells this in the *Politics*, Book I, and tells it precisely because people mocked philosophy as useless. Thales of Miletus, the story goes, read the signs and judged that the coming olive harvest would be enormous. In the dead of winter, when nobody wanted them, he put deposits on every olive press in Miletus and Chios, cheaply, because there was no competition for them.

The harvest came in huge. Everyone needed a press at once, and Thales rented them out on his own terms.

Aristotle's moral is double-edged. Philosophers could easily get rich if they thought it worth bothering with — and, he adds, what Thales really demonstrated is a general recipe that has nothing to do with wisdom: create a monopoly.

Treat it as an anecdote, not a record; Aristotle is writing two and a half centuries later. But look at the structure. Thales paid a small sum for the right, not the obligation, to use a press later at a fixed price. That is a call option, priced by a man who had no word for it.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What financial instrument does the Thales story describe?
A: A call option. He paid a small premium in winter for the right to use the presses at an agreed price at harvest, with no obligation if the crop failed. Aristotle reports it as proof that philosophers could get rich, and notes the real lesson is monopoly.
