# The shadow predicts the pole. Does it explain it?

Give me a flagpole's height and the sun's angle, and geometry gives me the shadow's length. Give me the shadow and the same angle, and geometry gives me the pole's height. Both deductions work. Yet the pole's height helps explain the shadow; the shadow does not explain why the pole has that height. Sylvain Bromberger used this case against the 1948 covering-law account of Carl Hempel and Paul Oppenheim, which treated explanation as deduction from laws and conditions. A valid calculation can run in either direction. An explanation needs something more, perhaps causal direction. When does an equation tell you *why*, rather than only what follows?

- [x] That explanation is asymmetric while deduction is not — so deducibility cannot be sufficient for explanation.
- [ ] That laws of nature are not universal enough to explain particular events — the laws used here work fine; the trouble is directionality.
