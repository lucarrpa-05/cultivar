# The word that got away from the man who coined it

Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* came out in 1962, oddly enough as a monograph in a logical-positivist encyclopedia series. Its picture: science is not a steady accumulation. Most of the time researchers do **normal science**, solving puzzles inside a shared framework and treating failures as their own fault rather than the framework's. Anomalies pile up, a crisis arrives, and a revolution installs a new framework whose practitioners ask different questions and no longer fully understand the old answers. That last part is **incommensurability**, and it is what made the book famous and suspect at once.

Then Margaret Masterman, in a 1970 paper, went through the text and counted at least twenty-one distinct senses of "paradigm" — from a whole worldview down to a single worked exemplar in a textbook. Kuhn accepted the hit and, in the 1970 postscript, split the word in two: the **disciplinary matrix** a community shares, and the **exemplars**, the concrete solved problems students learn by imitating.

The irony is perfect and instructive. A book arguing that terms quietly change meaning when frameworks shift had a term that changed meaning inside a single volume.
