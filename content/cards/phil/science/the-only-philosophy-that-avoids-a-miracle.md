# Why do theories work if their stories can be wrong?

Remember the interpretations of quantum mechanics: rival accounts of what the same successful mathematics describes. Scientific realism asks the wider question. If theories about unobservable electrons and fields predict and guide experiments so well, perhaps they are approximately true. Hilary Putnam argued in 1975 that realism alone keeps this success from looking miraculous. Larry Laudan answered with history: successful theories have later been rejected, sometimes along with the entities they proposed. Success therefore cannot straightforwardly guarantee truth. The tension is sharp. Should we believe a theory's picture of the world, or trust its predictions while holding that picture lightly?

## Rigor

**The no-miracles argument** is an inference to the best explanation: the novel predictive success of mature theories would be an inexplicable coincidence unless those theories were approximately true; so they probably are.

**The pessimistic meta-induction** attacks the premise linking success to truth. Laudan points to once-successful theories, including phlogiston and the luminiferous ether, whose proposed entities later lost their place in accepted science. If success has come apart from truth before, its connection to approximate truth needs a more careful defense.

The dialectic from there is worth knowing because it is a template. Realists retreat to **structural realism** (what survives theory change is the mathematical structure, not the ontology) or to **selective realism** (only the theoretical parts actually *doing the predicting* need be true — the ether was idle, Maxwell's equations were not). Anti-realists reply that the selection is made after the fact, which is exactly what a good prediction is not allowed to be.

Notice what this does to your quantum question. It stops being "which interpretation is right?" and becomes "what would even count as evidence that one is?"
