# Two questions that sound like one

Isaiah Berlin gave his inaugural lecture at Oxford on 31 October 1958 and separated two things the word *liberty* had been carrying at once.

**Negative liberty** answers: what is the area in which I am left alone? It is measured by absence — no one blocking the door, no law forbidding the book. **Positive liberty** answers a different question: who is master here? It is about self-direction, about being the author of your own life rather than a passenger.

Both sound good. Berlin's warning is about a slide in the second. If freedom is self-mastery, and the self that counts is the rational one, someone can claim to know what you would want if you were thinking clearly — and coercing you becomes liberating you. Berlin had watched that argument used by states that called their prisons schools.

He was not calling positive liberty worthless: the two are separate goods that genuinely conflict, which makes him a pluralist rather than a libertarian.

The counter-punch is fair. Charles Taylor argued in 1979 that pure negative liberty cannot tell a traffic light from a censorship law, since both are interferences.
