# The stranger on the broken bridge

The gentlest way into liberty is one stranger and one bridge. He is stepping onto a bridge you know is broken, no time to shout. You grab him. Did you take his freedom?

John Stuart Mill, in *On Liberty* (1859), said no: freedom is doing what you want, and "he does not desire to fall into the river." Change one detail: the bridge is only risky. Now, Mill says, you may only warn him: he alone can judge whether his reasons are worth the risk.

Isaiah Berlin's 1958 warning is about the other kind of grabber: people who claim to know what you *really* want, on questions murkier than a bridge.

Motorcycle helmet laws: grab, or warn?

Q: The bridge is only risky, not certainly broken. What does Mill let you do?
- [ ] Grab him anyway, since his safety matters more than his choice. — that is paternalism; Mill allows force only when you know he would not choose the outcome.
- [x] Warn him, then let him decide. — only he can weigh whether his reasons are worth the risk.
- [ ] Nothing, because even a warning interferes. — a warning adds information; it leaves the choice with him.
