# The voice recorded in 1860 and heard in 2008

Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville's phonautograph drew sound waves as lines on soot-darkened paper. It had no way to play them back. On 9 April 1860, someone sang *Au clair de la lune* into his machine, leaving a trace that spent generations silent in an archive. In 2008 researchers scanned that trace and converted the image into sound. The Library of Congress recognizes these phonautograms as early recordings of the human voice, made before Edison's playback-capable phonograph. Scott had built a way to *see* sound; digital tools supplied the missing way to hear it. The paper outlived the machine's original purpose.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why could Scott's 1860 phonautogram be heard in 2008 but not in 1860?
A: The phonautograph traced sound as a visual line on soot-blackened paper and had no playback mechanism at all — Scott wanted sound to be read, not heard. In 2008 the IRENE system at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory scanned those tracings as images and converted them into sound digitally.
