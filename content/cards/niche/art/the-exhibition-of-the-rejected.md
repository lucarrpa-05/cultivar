# The rejected paintings got their own exhibition

In 1863 the Paris Salon jury turned away more than half the submitted works. Artists complained, and Napoleon III authorized a separate display where the public could judge the refusals. The *Salon des Refusés* opened in May. Visitors encountered paintings that later became landmarks, including Manet's *Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe* and Whistler's *Symphony in White, No. 1*. Contemporary reactions included mockery, not instant vindication. The event mattered because official rejection became the exhibition's organizing idea: the public could see what the gatekeepers had excluded. It did not end the Salon's authority overnight, but it gave artists another way to seek an audience.

Q: Why did the Salon des Refusés matter?
