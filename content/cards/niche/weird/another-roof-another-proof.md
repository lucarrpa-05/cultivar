# He arrived at your door and said your brain was open

Paul Erdős (1913–1996) did mathematics the way other people are homeless. He owned a suitcase. He would appear at a colleague's house, work for a few days on whatever problem was in the air, and move on — "another roof, another proof". The lifestyle was not a quirk attached to the work; it *was* the work. Mathematics, for him, was a conversation you had to keep physically walking towards.

The output is barely believable: around 1,525 papers, more than any mathematician in history, with over 500 collaborators. That number is why the Erdős number exists — your collaboration distance from him, which several hundred thousand mathematicians can compute for themselves.

He had a private vocabulary for everything. God was the SF, the Supreme Fascist, who kept a Book containing the perfect proof of every theorem; the best compliment Erdős could pay an argument was that it came straight from the Book. Children were epsilons.

Paul Hoffman's 1998 biography calls him *The Man Who Loved Only Numbers*, which is almost right. What he loved was doing numbers *with* someone.

Q: What is an Erdős number, and why does the concept exist for him and not for other mathematicians?
A: It is your collaboration distance from Paul Erdős — 1 if you co-authored with him, 2 if you co-authored with one of his co-authors, and so on. It exists because with over 500 collaborators and around 1,525 papers he sits at the centre of the mathematical collaboration graph.
