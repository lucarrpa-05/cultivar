# He rebuilt geometry, then spent forty years walking away from it

Alexander Grothendieck (1928–2014) arrived at the IHÉS in 1958 and, over twelve years, rewrote algebraic geometry from the ground up — schemes, sheaf cohomology, the machinery that most of modern arithmetic geometry still runs on. Then, in 1970, he discovered the institute was partly funded by the military, and left.

He never really came back. In 1988 he refused the Crafoord Prize in an open letter, arguing that established mathematicians did not need more money and that the ethics of the scientific community were in decline. In 1991 he moved to Lasserre, a village in the Pyrenees, and gave his address to nobody.

The last act is the strangest. In January 2010 he wrote a *Déclaration d'intention de non-publication*: everything of his in circulation had been published without his permission, and he wanted all of it withdrawn — from libraries, from the web, everywhere.

It could not be done, of course. A mathematician's real work is in other people's heads, and there is no recall procedure for that. His refusal was total and, by construction, impossible.
