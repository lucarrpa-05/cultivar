# Fifteen hundred years ahead of schedule, then dropped in the sea

Sponge divers working a wreck off the Greek island of Antikythera in 1901 brought up statues, coins, and a shoebox-sized lump of corroded bronze that sat in a museum drawer while people argued about what it was.

It is a geared astronomical computer. Built in the late second or early first century BC, it carried some 35 meshing bronze gears, 30 of them still visible. Turn a crank and it gave the positions of the Sun and Moon through the zodiac, predicted eclipses decades ahead, tracked the Metonic cycle, and displayed the four-year cycle of athletic games. One gear train models the Moon's *irregular* orbit with an off-centre pin-and-slot.

Nothing of comparable mechanical complexity is known in Europe again until the astronomical clocks of the fourteenth century.

The unnerving part is not that the Greeks could do this. It is that we have exactly one of them. Whatever tradition produced it left no other surviving machine, which means our picture of ancient technology is drawn from a sample of one that happened to sink in the right place.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why does the Antikythera mechanism unsettle historians of technology rather than just impress them?
A: Because it is a sample of one. A device with 30-plus gears modelling the Moon's irregular motion cannot have been a first attempt, yet no comparable machine survives from antiquity — and nothing of that complexity appears again in Europe until the fourteenth century. What else existed, we have no way of knowing.
