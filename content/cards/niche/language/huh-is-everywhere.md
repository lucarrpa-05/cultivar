# The one word almost every language agrees on

Words are supposed to be arbitrary. *Dog*, *perro*, *inu* — no reason for any of them, which is why unrelated languages sound nothing alike. Then there is *huh?*

Mark Dingemanse, Francisco Torreira and N. J. Enfield recorded ordinary conversation in ten languages from five continents — Siwu in Ghana, Cha'palaa in Ecuador, Murriny Patha in Australia, Icelandic, Mandarin, Spanish — and found the same item in each: a short, questioning syllable, low front vowel, no consonants to speak of, rising intonation. Close enough that the similarity is far beyond chance.

Their explanation is not shared ancestry, and not instinct. It is convergent evolution. Every conversation on Earth faces the same engineering problem: you missed something, and you have a fraction of a second to say so before the speaker moves on. The solution has to be minimal — one syllable, producible with a half-open mouth, unmistakably a question. Selection pressure that specific yields the same shape everywhere.

And the second claim of the paper is the subtler one.

## Rigor

The authors argue *huh?* is a **word**, not a grunt, and the distinction is testable. A cry of pain or a sneeze is involuntary and its form is not language-specific. A word is conventional, learned, and fitted to the sound system of its language.

Their evidence: the vowel in *huh?* is not identical across languages but varies *within each language's own vowel inventory*, and the intonation follows the language's own question prosody — rising in English and Spanish, falling in Icelandic, where questions fall. A reflex would not be locally adapted like that. Children have to learn it.

So the argument is a two-sided one, and both sides matter: the *function* is universal because the interactional problem is universal, while the *form* is conventional because it has been fitted to each language. The similarity is not inheritance from a common ancestor but the same problem solved the same way, independently, thousands of times — the linguistic version of the eye evolving again and again.

Q: What is the evidence that *huh?* is a learned word rather than an involuntary noise?
- [ ] It appears in every recorded language — universality is compatible with a reflex; sneezes are universal too.
- [x] Its vowel and intonation are fitted to each language's own sound system and question prosody — a reflex would not vary language by language in exactly that way.
- [ ] It is written down in dictionaries — orthography reflects convention about spelling, not whether the item is linguistic.
