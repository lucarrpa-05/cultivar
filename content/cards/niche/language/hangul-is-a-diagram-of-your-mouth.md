# The alphabet that draws the inside of your mouth

Latin letters are the descendants of pictures of things — A was an ox head, once, upside down. They tell you nothing about the sound. Hangul does something no other major script does: the letter *is* a diagram of the organ making the noise.

Announced around 1443 and published in 1446 under Sejong the Great, its five basic consonants each depict an articulation. ㄱ shows the back of the tongue blocking the soft palate, which is exactly what you do to say *k*. ㅁ is a mouth, and it is *m*. ㄴ is the tongue tip touching behind the teeth, *n*. Then the system compounds: add a stroke for more breath, so ㄷ becomes ㅌ, plain *t* becoming aspirated *t*.

The companion volume, the *Hunminjeongeum Haerye*, explains all of this in writing — which makes Hangul one of the very few scripts in history whose designers left behind their reasoning.

Sejong's stated motive was blunt: Korean sounds do not fit Chinese characters, and ordinary people could not learn them. It was engineering aimed at literacy, and it worked.
