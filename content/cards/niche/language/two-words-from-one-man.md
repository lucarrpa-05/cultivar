# One man in Baghdad named two-thirds of your maths vocabulary

Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī, working in Baghdad sometime between 813 and 833, wrote a book with *al-jabr* in the title. The word did not mean anything mathematical yet: *al-jabr* was what a surgeon did to a broken bone — forcing the parts back together. Al-Khwārizmī borrowed it for what you do to an equation when you move a term across the equals sign and make the pieces whole again.

Latin translators turned *al-jabr* into *algebra* and kept it.

Then they did it again with his name. Al-Khwārizmī also wrote the manual that taught Europe to calculate with Hindu–Arabic numerals instead of Roman ones, and the Latin versions open with *Algoritmi* — his name, mangled. The step-by-step method in the book took on the name of the man: *algorism*, eventually *algorithm*.

So every time you say "algorithm" you are mispronouncing a place name — al-Khwārizmī means "the man from Khwarazm", a region south of the Aral Sea. Twelve centuries later it is one of the most-used words in technology.

type: reveal
Q: Where do the words "algebra" and "algorithm" come from, and what did *al-jabr* originally mean?
A: Both trace to one 9th-century scholar. *Algebra* comes from *al-jabr* in the title of his book — a word that originally meant bone-setting, the forcing back together of broken parts. *Algorithm* comes from the Latinisation of his own name, *Algoritmi*.
