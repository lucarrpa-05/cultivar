# A man who could not read invented a writing system

Every other script on Earth was adapted from an older script, or built by people who already knew how writing worked. The Cherokee syllabary is the exception that historians keep coming back to: Sequoyah was illiterate in every language, including his own, when he started.

He had seen white settlers reading — "talking leaves" — and reasoned from the outside that marks could hold speech. Through the late 1810s he tried a symbol per word, which failed under its own weight, then switched to a symbol per syllable. Cherokee has few enough syllables for that to work. He ended with 86 characters, later trimmed to 85 for the printing press.

The adoption is the astonishing part. By 1825 the majority of Cherokee could read and write in it, a language that had been unwritten a decade earlier. On 21 February 1828 the *Cherokee Phoenix* began publishing at New Echota in Cherokee and English, the first newspaper in a Native American language.

One man, working alone, compressed several thousand years of writing's development into about a decade.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why was a syllabary — rather than an alphabet — the right design for Cherokee?
A: Sequoyah first tried one symbol per word and drowned in symbols. Cherokee has a small enough inventory of syllables that one symbol per syllable is manageable: 86 characters covered the language, and learners could start reading almost immediately without analysing sounds below the syllable.
