# The board game designed as propaganda against itself

In 1904 Elizabeth "Lizzie" Magie patented The Landlord's Game. She was a follower of Henry George, who argued that land rent enriches owners without their producing anything, and she built the board as an argument: go round, land on other people's property, pay, get poorer, watch one player end up with everything.

The design move that makes her a serious game designer is the second rule set. The same board could be played two ways. Under the monopolist rules you win by bankrupting everyone. Under the anti-monopolist rules, rents fed a common pot and every player got richer together. You played both, and the point was the comparison.

Parker Brothers bought Monopoly in the 1930s from Charles Darrow, who claimed to have invented it. In 1935 they bought Magie's patent too, for \$500, pushed her game aside within a year, and in 1939 printed a token run of it to secure the rights.

So the world's best-known board game is a teaching tool with its lesson removed — and the reason everyone finds Monopoly miserable and interminable is that it is *working exactly as designed*.

type: reveal
Q: What did Lizzie Magie's original 1904 game include that Monopoly does not?
A: A second, anti-monopolist rule set for the same board, in which rents went to a common pot and all players prospered together. Playing both versions was the point: the contrast was the argument for Henry George's land-value tax.
