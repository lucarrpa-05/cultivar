# The Enlightenment was sold on subscription

The first edition of Diderot and d'Alembert's *Encyclopédie* began appearing in 1751. Its 17 text volumes and 11 volumes of plates cost about 980 livres a set. Buyers subscribed in advance, paying for a work that would take two decades to finish.

That expensive edition sold 4,225 copies and earned its publishers more than two million livres. Cheaper editions followed. By 1789 roughly 25,000 sets in various formats had sold across Europe. Historian Robert Darnton's point is that Enlightenment ideas needed more than brilliant authors: printers, booksellers, subscribers and a business model carried them. The subscription list helped turn a vast manuscript project into something people could actually read.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why does Darnton study the Encyclopédie's accounts rather than only its arguments?
A: Because the accounts explain the reach. A 4,225-copy first edition at 980 livres was an elite object; the cheaper quarto and octavo reprints put it within the upper middle class, and about 25,000 sets had sold across Europe by 1789.
