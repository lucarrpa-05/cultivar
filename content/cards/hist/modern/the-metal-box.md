# A trucker got bored waiting at the docks

Malcom McLean ran a trucking company in North Carolina. The story he told was of sitting in a queue at a Hoboken pier in 1937, watching longshoremen move his cargo one sack at a time, and wondering why the whole trailer could not simply be lifted aboard. Marc Levinson, who wrote the book on this, calls that origin story possibly apocryphal.

On 26 April 1956 a converted tanker, the *Ideal-X*, left Newark for Houston carrying 58 metal boxes on its deck. Loading a ship by hand cost about \$5.83 a ton at the time. With containers it cost 15.8 cents. That is not an improvement; it is a different industry.

Everything downstream followed: cranes, standard sizes, ports moving from crowded city waterfronts to open land, dockworker unions gutted, and the cost of distance falling far enough that manufacturing could be split across oceans. Nothing was invented that a nineteenth-century engineer could not have built. The hard part was getting railways, truckers, ports and unions to agree on a box.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why is the container usually credited with more than the ships that carry it?
A: Because the binding cost was handling, not sailing. Loading by hand ran around \$5.83 a ton; containerised, 15.8 cents. Collapsing that cost is what made splitting production across oceans worth doing.
