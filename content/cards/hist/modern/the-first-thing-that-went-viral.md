# The first thing that ever went viral

Jan Hus said most of it a century earlier and was burned at Constance in 1415. Martin Luther posted his ninety-five theses on 31 October 1517 — the nailing to the church door is a later embellishment, reported in 1546 by Melanchthon, who did not reach Wittenberg until 1518 — and within weeks the text was in print across the German lands, in Latin and then in German.

The machinery mattered more than the argument. Luther wrote short, cheap, furious pamphlets in the vernacular, and printers, who were businesses, discovered he sold. A trade built on slow Latin bibles became a news industry.

You can test this rather than assert it. Jared Rubin, in 2014, compared German-speaking cities by whether they had a press by 1500, using distance from Mainz — Gutenberg's town — as the instrument. Cities with a press by 1500 were, in Rubin's words, at minimum 29 percentage points more likely to be Protestant by 1600.

The lesson generalises uncomfortably. An idea's spread is not determined only by its truth or its appeal, but by whether the reproduction technology of the moment happens to favour its format.
