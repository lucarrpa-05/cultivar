# The wages that would not stay down

Between 1347 and 1351 somewhere between a third and sixty per cent of Europe died, wildly unevenly by region. England went from about 4.8 million people in 1348 to about 2.1 million by 1400. Whole villages were abandoned.

The survivors inherited an economy with the same fields, the same ploughs, the same mills and far fewer hands. Labour became scarce and therefore expensive, and tenants who could walk to a better manor did. Landlords reacted the way people with political power react: England issued the Ordinance of Labourers in 1349 and the Statute of Labourers in 1351, forbidding any worker to demand more than the wages of 1346.

That law is the good evidence. Nobody legislates against a wage rise that is not happening. The statutes were enforced and resented for thirty years, a standing grievance behind the Peasants' Revolt of 1381 — though what actually lit that was the poll tax.

Whether the gains lasted, and how they diverged, is where historians stop agreeing.

## Rigor

Two claims need separating, and the popular version blurs them. **Nominal** wages jumped at once, 1348 to 1350, and then crawled: Gregory Clark's series has English farm labour at 3.08d a day in 1350 and 3.66d in 1450, under twenty per cent across a century. The bigger move is in **real** wages, which John Munro's estimates put at roughly two to three times their pre-plague level by the mid-fifteenth century — because grain prices fell while money wages held.

The deeper dispute, from the Brenner debate of the 1970s onward, is about why the same shock produced opposite institutions. Western Europe saw serfdom decay into cash rents and copyhold tenure; eastern Europe saw lords tighten the law and bind peasants harder, the so-called second serfdom. Identical demographic shock, opposite outcome, so demography alone cannot be the explanation. Brenner's answer was the prior balance of class power and the density of peasant organisation; his critics point to land-labour ratios, urbanisation and the market for grain.

The honest summary: the Black Death is the best natural experiment in pre-modern economic history, and it does not identify a single effect. It identifies an interaction between a shock and whatever institutions were standing when it hit.
