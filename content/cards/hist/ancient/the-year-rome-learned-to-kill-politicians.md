# The year Rome learned to kill its politicians

In 133 BCE, tribune Tiberius Gracchus proposed enforcing an old limit on occupation of public land and redistributing the surplus. Fellow tribune Marcus Octavius vetoed it. Tiberius then had Octavius deposed, a step with no Roman precedent. At a later electoral assembly, senators and their supporters beat Tiberius and many followers to death; their bodies were thrown into the Tiber.

Historians often treat 133 BCE as a hinge in the Republic's decline. The reform itself was not the only break. A tribune was removed to pass a law, then a political dispute ended in organized killing. Tiberius's brother Gaius would die amid another confrontation twelve years later.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why do historians treat 133 BC as the start of the Republic's decline rather than just a failed reform?
A: Because it broke a norm. Tiberius removed a vetoing tribune without constitutional warrant, and the senate's answer was lethal violence at an assembly. Both moves had no precedent, and both were repeated for the next century.
