# The plan that lost at Bretton Woods

From 1 to 22 July 1944, with the war still running, 730 delegates from 44 nations met at a hotel in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, to design the monetary system of a world that did not exist yet.

Two plans were on the table. John Maynard Keynes proposed an International Clearing Union with its own unit of account, the *bancor*, and one radical feature: it charged countries that ran persistent surpluses as well as those that ran deficits. If Germany sells far more than it buys, someone else must be borrowing to buy it, and Keynes wanted the adjustment shared. Harry Dexter White, for the United States, proposed a fund where deficit countries adjust and surplus countries accumulate as they please.

The United States held the gold and the creditor position, so White won. The result was the IMF, the World Bank, currencies pegged to a dollar pegged to gold, and an asymmetry that has never gone away.

The trouble with the winning plan was visible before it collapsed.

## Rigor

Two constraints explain both the system and its end.

**The trilemma.** A country cannot simultaneously have a fixed exchange rate, free capital mobility and an independent monetary policy; it must pick two. Bretton Woods chose the fixed rate and monetary independence, and paid for them with capital controls — which is why the era looks so orderly, and why liberalising capital in the 1960s destabilised it.

**The Triffin dilemma.** If one country's currency is the world's reserve asset, the world's demand for reserves grows with world trade. The issuer can only supply them by running balance-of-payments deficits. But the more dollars are held abroad relative to the gold backing them, the less credible convertibility at $\$35$ per ounce becomes. Supply the world and lose credibility; protect credibility and starve the world of liquidity. Robert Triffin put this to Congress on 28 October 1959, and to everyone else in *Gold and the Dollar Crisis* the year after.

Both ends duly arrived. On 15 August 1971 Nixon suspended convertibility, and by 1973 the major currencies floated. Keynes's bancor was an attempt to dodge Triffin by making the reserve asset belong to no one — which is roughly what the IMF's Special Drawing Rights, created in 1969, tried and mostly failed to be.
