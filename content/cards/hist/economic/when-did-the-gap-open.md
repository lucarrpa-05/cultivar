# When did the gap actually open?

Kenneth Pomeranz argued in 2000 that the Yangzi delta and Europe's leading regions had comparable living standards as late as the eighteenth century. If so, Britain's nearby coal and access to New World resources could explain a relatively late break.

Stephen Broadberry and Bishnupriya Gupta found a different picture in early modern wage data: workers in northwestern Europe earned much more silver than workers in advanced Asian regions, even when grain wages looked closer. That suggests an earlier gap. The quarrel is partly about what counts as a living standard. Silver buys traded goods; grain wages speak more directly to food. Change the yardstick and you move the historical turning point.

## Rigor

The dispute turns on what a wage comparison across continents means. Broadberry and Gupta compare silver wages, which measure purchasing power over internationally traded goods, and grain wages, which better reflect access to a basic staple. Neither alone measures total household welfare: prices, work days and non-market production differ across regions.

The alternative is a **welfare ratio**: price a fixed basket of local subsistence goods in each place, and express the wage as a multiple of it. Do that and the Chinese-European gap narrows sharply, though it does not vanish, and the choice of basket now carries the conclusion — a bare-bones grain basket and a respectable basket give different answers about the same year.

Everyone agrees on a large nineteenth-century divergence. Whether leading regions were comparable around 1700–1750 remains disputed. A later break gives more explanatory weight to coal and colonial resources; an earlier one invites explanations with longer histories. The yardstick is therefore part of the argument, not a neutral preliminary.
