# Remember what money is? Hungary, 1946, says it again

Hyperinflation has a record holder, and it is Hungary after the war: by July 1946 prices were doubling roughly every fifteen hours. The largest note actually issued was 100 million *b.-pengő*, which is a 1 followed by twenty zeros; a note for ten times more was printed and never released. On 1 August 1946 the forint replaced the pengő at a rate of 400 octillion to one. Photographs from January 1946 show banknotes left lying uncollected on the Danube bank.

This is the cleanest demonstration of what money is. Not a substance. A shared bet that somebody else will accept the thing tomorrow. Once the bet fails, people spend faster, the same notes chase goods more often, and prices climb faster than the printing — which is why hyperinflations accelerate instead of settling.

The ending is the part worth carrying. Hyperinflations do not fade out; they stop, often within days. Thomas Sargent's study of four 1920s cases argued the common ingredient is not slow monetary tightening but a credible change of fiscal regime: the government visibly stops needing the printing press. Expectations, not the money stock alone, are what break.
