# The metre is 0.2 millimetres wrong, and he knew

In 1791 revolutionary France decided the new unit of length should belong to nobody: one ten-millionth of the distance from the North Pole to the equator, along the meridian through Paris. Two astronomers were sent to measure it. Jean-Baptiste Delambre took the northern arc, Dunkirk down to Rodez; Pierre Méchain took the southern, Rodez to Barcelona. Both worked through a country at war, arrested repeatedly as suspected spies, carrying instruments that looked exactly like a spy's.

Seven years later Méchain had a problem. His two sets of latitude readings at Barcelona disagreed by more than his instruments should allow. He did not report it. He revised, withheld, agonised, and went back to Spain to fix the arc, where he died in September 1804 of a fever, usually identified as yellow fever.

The metre that came out of the survey is about 0.197 mm shorter than the definition it was chasing, mostly because the survey ignored the deflection of the vertical at its southern end — a gravitational quirk, not Méchain's carelessness. His secret was a real error hiding inside a systematic one.

Q: Why is the metre not exactly one ten-millionth of the pole-to-equator distance?
A: The 1792-99 meridian survey carried a small systematic error, chiefly from ignoring the deflection of the vertical near its southern end, leaving the metre about 0.197 mm short. The unit was kept anyway, and later redefined against a bar, then a wavelength, then the speed of light.
