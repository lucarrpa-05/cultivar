# Fifteen rows, and no label saying why

Plimpton 322 is a clay tablet written in southern Mesopotamia around 1800 BCE. Its fifteen surviving rows contain numbers connected to right triangles: with the right interpretation, their sides satisfy the rule later associated with Pythagoras. The puzzle is why a scribe wanted this table.

In 1945 Neugebauer and Sachs read it as a deliberate collection of numerical triples. Eleanor Robson argued in 2001 that its reciprocal calculations fit scribal teaching practices better. A 2017 paper revived a trigonometric reading, which other historians dispute as anachronistic. The numbers survive; the tablet supplies no caption explaining its purpose. That gap is a reminder that understanding old mathematics takes more than spotting a familiar equation.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What is actually agreed about Plimpton 322, and what is not?
A: The numerical entries can be read as right-triangle triples. Their purpose is disputed: numerical exploration, a scribal teaching aid, and a trigonometric reading have all been proposed. Robson argues that the last imports a later concept of angle.
