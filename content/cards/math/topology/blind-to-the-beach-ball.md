# The fundamental group is blind to a beach ball

Take any loop on the surface of a sphere and slide it around. It always shrinks to a point — go around the equator, then slip the loop over the north pole and pull it tight. So $\pi_1(S^2)$ is trivial, the same answer you get for a solid disk, or for a single point.

But $S^2$ is not a point. It encloses something; it has a hole. Loops simply cannot catch that kind of hole, because a hole of dimension two needs a two-dimensional probe. Wrap a *sphere* around the sphere instead of a loop and you get an invariant that counts: $\pi_2(S^2)\cong\mathbb{Z}$, and likewise $H_2(S^2)\cong\mathbb{Z}$.

Then the subject falls off a cliff. You would expect maps from a 3-sphere to a 2-sphere to be uninteresting — too many dimensions to fit. In 1931 Heinz Hopf produced one that cannot be deformed to a constant, and in fact $\pi_3(S^2)\cong\mathbb{Z}$. Higher homotopy groups of spheres have been computed one by one ever since, and there is no general answer.

## Rigor

$\pi_n(X,x_0)$ is the set of homotopy classes of maps $(S^n, \text{pt})\to(X,x_0)$; it is a group for $n\ge1$ and abelian for $n\ge2$.

$\pi_1(S^2)=0$: a loop can be homotoped to miss a point $q$ (subdivide by a Lebesgue number and replace each small piece by an arc), and $S^2\setminus\{q\}\cong\mathbb{R}^2$ is contractible.

The **Hopf fibration** $S^1 \hookrightarrow S^3 \xrightarrow{h} S^2$ sends $(z_1,z_2)$ with $|z_1|^2+|z_2|^2=1$ to $z_1/z_2 \in \mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}\cong S^2$; fibres are circles, and any two of them are linked. Its long exact sequence of homotopy groups gives
$$\pi_n(S^3)\to\pi_n(S^2)\to\pi_{n-1}(S^1)$$
with $\pi_n(S^1)=0$ for $n\ge2$, so $\pi_3(S^2)\cong\pi_3(S^3)\cong\mathbb{Z}$, generated by $h$ itself.

Homology behaves better: $H_k(S^n)=\mathbb{Z}$ for $k=0,n$ and $0$ otherwise, computable for every $n$ at once. That is the trade. Homotopy groups carry more information and are mostly unknown; homology carries less and can always be computed.

## Recall
type: mcq
Q: $\pi_1(S^2)$ is trivial. What does that tell you about $S^2$?
- [x] Only that loops detect nothing there — a 2-dimensional hole needs a 2-dimensional probe, and $\pi_2(S^2)\cong H_2(S^2)\cong\mathbb{Z}$ finds it.
- [ ] That $S^2$ is contractible — it is not; if it were, all its homology would vanish, and $H_2(S^2)=\mathbb{Z}$.
- [ ] That $S^2$ has no holes of any kind — it encloses a cavity, which is exactly the hole that the second homotopy and homology groups pick up.
