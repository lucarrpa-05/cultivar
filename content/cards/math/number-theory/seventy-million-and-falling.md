# Seventy million, and falling

Twin primes — 11 and 13, 17 and 19, 101 and 103 — keep turning up in prime tables, and nobody could prove they keep turning up forever. Not "probably infinitely many". Nobody could prove there is *any* fixed distance $d$ such that infinitely many pairs of primes sit exactly $d$ apart. For 150 years, the gap between primes was allowed, as far as anyone could show, to grow without bound.

On 17 April 2013 Yitang Zhang submitted a paper proving there is such a $d$, and it is at most 70 million.

Zhang was 58, a lecturer at the University of New Hampshire, and had not published a paper since 2001. After his doctorate he had worked as an accountant, in a motel, and in a Subway sandwich shop. The *Annals of Mathematics* accepted the paper in weeks.

Seventy million is a comic bound and everybody knew it. James Maynard and Terence Tao, independently of each other, found a simpler and stronger method, and within a year the Polymath collaboration had ground the bound down to 246. Twin primes need 2.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What exactly did Zhang prove, and what is still open?
A: That there exists a finite $d\le 70{,}000{,}000$ with infinitely many prime pairs differing by exactly $d$ — the first proof that prime gaps do not always grow. The bound is now 246, via Maynard and Polymath8. The twin prime conjecture, $d=2$, is still open, and the current methods appear to stall at 6.
