# Hilbert's proof was called theology. Nobody wrote that down until 1914.

In 1888 David Hilbert proved that certain systems of polynomial invariants have finitely many generators, without exhibiting them. Legend says Paul Gordan, the king of explicit invariant calculations, snapped: this is not mathematics, it is theology.

Colin McLarty traced the line. It first appears in 1914, a quarter century later, in Max Noether's eulogy of Gordan. At the time Gordan had supported the proof; he only wanted it developed further before it went into *Mathematische Annalen*.

The twist: Max's daughter Emmy was Gordan's only doctoral student, became the great champion of Hilbert's style, and kept a framed picture of Gordan in her Göttingen office.

Her name now labels the finiteness Hilbert proved.

## Rigor

An $R$-module $M$ is **Noetherian** if every submodule is finitely generated; equivalently, every ascending chain of submodules $N_1\subseteq N_2\subseteq\cdots$ is eventually constant. A ring is Noetherian when it is Noetherian as a module over itself.

**Hilbert's basis theorem.** If $R$ is Noetherian, so is $R[x]$. Hence $k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ is Noetherian for any field $k$.

*Sketch.* For an ideal $I\subseteq R[x]$, the leading coefficients of degree-$d$ elements of $I$ form ideals $L_0\subseteq L_1\subseteq\cdots$ of $R$. The chain stabilises and each $L_d$ is finitely generated; polynomials realising those finitely many leading coefficients generate $I$, by reducing degrees one step at a time. Nothing is computed, which was exactly the point of the quarrel.

**For modules.** Over a Noetherian ring, every submodule of a finitely generated module is finitely generated.

**Without the hypothesis it fails.** $R=k[x_1,x_2,\dots]$ is generated by one element as a module over itself, namely $1$. Its submodule $(x_1,x_2,\dots)$ needs infinitely many generators: finitely many of them involve only $x_1,\dots,x_N$, and sending $x_1,\dots,x_N$ to $0$ kills all of them but not $x_{N+1}$.

## Recall
type: mcq
Q: What did Hilbert's 1888 proof establish that later became the stuff of legend?
- [x] That a finite generating set exists, without exhibiting one — an existence proof where a calculation was expected.
- [ ] That invariants cannot be computed — they can, and Gordan had computed many; Hilbert simply did not need to.
- [ ] That polynomial rings have infinitely generated ideals — the opposite: every ideal of $k[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ is finitely generated.
