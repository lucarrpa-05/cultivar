# The letter that broke a book already at the printer's

In June 1902 Bertrand Russell posted a short letter to Gottlob Frege, whose second volume on the foundations of arithmetic was in press. Russell had one question.

Frege's system let you collect all the things with a given property into a set. Fine. So collect all the sets that are *not* members of themselves. Is that collection a member of itself?

If it is, then by its own definition it isn't. If it isn't, then it is.

Frege replied on 22 June and wrote an appendix for a volume he could no longer stop. It opens: "Hardly anything more unfortunate can befall a scientific writer than to have one of the foundations of his edifice shaken after the work is finished."

Russell's popular version needs no symbols. A barber shaves exactly those men who don't shave themselves. Who shaves the barber? The repair took thirty years, and it is why "set" is now a word with rules attached.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What exactly did Russell's question destroy?
A: The idea that every property defines a set. "The set of all sets that are not members of themselves" both is and is not a member of itself, so unrestricted comprehension is inconsistent — later systems only let you carve subsets out of sets you already have.
