# The lion and his claw

In June 1696 Johann Bernoulli published a challenge in the *Acta Eruditorum*, addressed to the sharpest mathematicians in the world. Given two points at different heights, what shape of wire gets a bead from one to the other fastest under gravity? Not the straight line — a steeper start buys speed that pays for the longer path. But which curve?

He set a six-month deadline, then extended it to Easter 1697 at Leibniz's urging.

According to Newton's niece Catherine Conduitt, he came home from the Mint at four in the afternoon on 29 January 1697, found the problem waiting, and had it by four the next morning. He sent it in without his name on it.

Bernoulli knew anyway. In a letter that March he wrote that he recognised the author as one recognises the lion by its claw — the polished *tanquam ex ungue leonem* everyone repeats is David Brewster's, added in his 1855 life of Newton.

The curve is the cycloid — the one Huygens had already met for a different reason.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What is the brachistochrone, and what is the twist about the same curve?
A: The cycloid - the path traced by a point on a rolling circle. The twist is that Huygens had shown in 1659 that the cycloid is also the tautochrone: a bead released from any point on it arrives at the bottom in the same time.
