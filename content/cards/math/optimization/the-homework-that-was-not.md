# The homework that was not homework

In 1939 a graduate student at Berkeley came in late to Jerzy Neyman's statistics class and copied two problems off the blackboard, assuming they were the week's assignment. They were harder than usual. He handed in solutions to both a few days afterwards, apologising for the delay.

About six weeks later Neyman turned up at his door, excited. The two problems on the board had not been homework. They were two well-known unsolved problems in statistics.

Neyman prepared one of the solutions for publication. The other had a sequel: when Abraham Wald was about to publish the same result, he learned of the earlier proof and simply added the student's name to the paper.

The student was George Dantzig, who eight years later produced the simplex algorithm. The story then had a second life it did not need: it was recycled as a positive-thinking parable, drifted from the facts, and turned up as the opening scene of *Good Will Hunting* in 1997. The documented version is better, and it is documented.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What is the part of the Dantzig story that usually gets distorted?
A: The moral. It is told as proof that believing a problem is easy makes it solvable. What actually happened is that a strong student, not knowing the problems had a reputation, spent a few days on two hard but genuinely tractable questions.
