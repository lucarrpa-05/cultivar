# Biologists have at least two dozen definitions of "species"

Dogs and cats are easy to separate. At the edges of evolving lineages, the word *species* gets harder.

One rule groups populations that can breed with one another but are reproductively isolated from others. That helps with many sexual organisms; it cannot be applied directly to fossils or organisms that reproduce without sex. Another rule asks whether a lineage has a distinctive combination of traits. An ecological rule asks whether it occupies a distinct niche.

These rules can cut the same family tree differently. A finer rule may raise a species count without discovering a single new organism. The lineages are real; drawing their boundaries requires a choice about which evidence matters.

## Rigor

Counts depend on how finely one distinguishes concepts. Mayden listed 22 definitions; Wilkins later counted 26 in use since the Modern Synthesis and grouped them into seven basic kinds: agamospecies, biospecies, ecospecies, evolutionary, genetic, morphospecies and taxonomic species.

Two of them, stated properly:

**Biological species concept** (Mayr, 1942): groups of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations, reproductively isolated from other such groups.

**Diagnosability-based concept**: a lineage distinguishable from others by a combination of inherited characters. Different phylogenetic species concepts make this criterion more or less strict.

The second makes no reference to breeding, so it can be used with fossils or asexual lineages. It may split populations that a reproductive criterion groups together.

The practical fallout includes different species counts and conservation priorities. Neither a single gene tree nor a single breeding test settles every boundary: gene flow, ancestry, and ecological differences can disagree. Species delimitation therefore weighs several lines of evidence, and the answer can change when better data arrive.
