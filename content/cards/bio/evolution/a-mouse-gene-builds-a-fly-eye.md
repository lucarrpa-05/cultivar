# A mouse gene, dropped into a fly, orders an eye

In 1995 Walter Gehring's lab in Basel forced a fly gene called *eyeless* to switch on in tissue that was supposed to become a leg, or a wing, or an antenna. The flies grew eyes there: structured, pigmented compound eyes, in the wrong places.

Then the result that still sounds made up. The mouse version of that gene, *Pax6*, does the same job in a fly. A gene from a mammal, expressed in a fly's leg, triggers the construction of a *fly's* compound eye — not a mouse eye, not a hybrid. The instruction and the machinery it calls are separable, and the instruction is old enough to be shared by animals whose last common ancestor may not have had eyes worth the name.

That separation is the whole thesis of evolutionary developmental biology: big changes in form often come from switching ancient genes on at new times and places, not from inventing new genes.

## Rigor

*Pax6* encodes a transcription factor with two DNA-binding domains, a paired domain and a homeodomain. It sits near the top of a regulatory network — *eyes absent*, *sine oculis*, *dachshund* and others — with feedback among the members. Calling it "the master control gene for the eye" is a simplification Gehring himself argued about: it is sufficient to launch the cascade in competent tissue, which is not the same as being the thing that specifies an eye.

The precise claim is **deep homology**. Fly and vertebrate eyes are not homologous as organs; they are built from different tissues on different plans. What is homologous is the genetic switch that initiates them. Conservation lives in the circuit, not in the product.

The same pattern shows up at body scale in the *Hox* genes, a cluster of transcription factors with a property that has no obvious reason to be true: they are arranged along the chromosome in the same order as the body regions they specify, head to tail. That spatial colinearity holds in flies and in us, which is why an ordering on a piece of DNA is also a map of an animal.
