# Crick picked the wrong word, and biology kept it anyway

He meant something like "a big idea nobody has evidence for yet". He wrote in 1988 that he had not understood that a dogma is a belief that *cannot be doubted*, and that he could just as well have called it the Central Hypothesis.

The idea itself is simple and, unlike the name, careful. DNA is a written record. To use a stretch of it, the cell copies that stretch into RNA — transcription — and a ribosome reads the RNA three letters at a time, adding one amino acid per triplet, building a protein. Sequence in, sequence out, one letter at a time.

The claim is about the *direction*. Information flows from nucleic acid into protein and never back out. A protein cannot dictate a sequence of DNA. That is why an animal's experiences do not get written into its genome, and why Lamarck stays dead: not because acquired traits are absurd, but because there is no machine that could write them down.

## Rigor

Crick's 1958 statement is sharper than the cartoon arrow chain: once information has passed into protein, it cannot get out again. Transfers between nucleic acids, and from nucleic acid to protein, are possible; transfers from protein to protein or protein to nucleic acid are not.

Sort the nine possible transfers among DNA, RNA and protein into three classes. General, seen in every cell: DNA to DNA, DNA to RNA, RNA to protein. Special, real but restricted: RNA to DNA (reverse transcriptase, used by retroviruses and by your own retrotransposons), RNA to RNA (many RNA viruses), and DNA straight to protein (in cell-free systems). Forbidden, never observed: protein to DNA, protein to RNA, protein to protein.

So reverse transcription, discovered in 1970 and often sold as "the central dogma was wrong", is a *special* transfer that Crick had already allowed. The forbidden row is the one that has never fallen — and it is the row that makes heredity one-directional.

Q: Does reverse transcriptase — RNA copied back into DNA — break the central dogma?
- [x] No — the rule forbids information leaving protein, and Crick listed RNA-to-DNA as a possible special transfer from the start.
- [ ] Yes: it reverses the arrow, so the dogma is false as stated — the dogma was never a claim that arrows only point one way among nucleic acids.
