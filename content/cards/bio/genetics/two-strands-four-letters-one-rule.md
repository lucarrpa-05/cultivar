# Two strands, four letters, and one rule that does all the work

Imagine DNA flattened into a ladder. Its sugar-phosphate backbones are the rails; the rungs are pairs of bases. The sequence of those bases carries the inherited instructions.

The pairing rule is simple: A usually pairs with T, and G with C. So one strand specifies the complementary sequence of the other. Separate the strands and each can guide the construction of a new partner.

That does not mean copying happens by itself or without mistakes. Enzymes open the helix, add matching bases, and proofread many errors. The elegance is that a strand can serve as both stored information and a physical template for copying it.

## Rigor

A–T pairs form two hydrogen bonds; G–C pairs form three. Base stacking, salt, and sequence context also affect helix stability, so the hydrogen-bond count alone does not predict melting temperature.

The strands run **antiparallel**. One goes 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′, numbering carbon positions on the sugar. Polymerases can only add to a 3′ end, so the two strands cannot be copied the same way — one is built continuously, the other in backward fragments.

For ordinary double-stranded DNA, complementarity also explains Chargaff's observation that total A approximately equals T and G approximately equals C. The rule does not apply in the same way to single-stranded genomes. At a replication fork, opposite strand orientations force one daughter strand to be made continuously and the other in short fragments. The polymerase and repair machinery improve accuracy, but occasional errors become mutations.
