# Two metres of thread in a six-micron room, and it never knots

Stretch the DNA in one of your cells end to end and you get about two metres. It lives inside a nucleus roughly six thousandths of a millimetre across. Scale that up and you have twenty kilometres of fishing line stuffed into a tennis ball. Now copy every centimetre of it and drag the two copies to opposite ends of the room without leaving them linked.

This is a topology problem, and cells solve it with topology tools. Enzymes called topoisomerases cut the double helix, let another strand pass through the gap, and seal it again. Cut, pass, reseal: that is exactly the crossing change a knot theorist draws on paper when simplifying a tangle.

The type II enzymes do the heavy version. They cut both strands of one helix and pass an entire second helix through the hole, which is how two finished chromosomes, wound around each other after copying, finally come apart. Jam those enzymes and division stalls — which is precisely what several chemotherapy drugs are built to do.
