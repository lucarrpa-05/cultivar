# Every cell you own holds back a lightning-strength field

Take the voltage across a resting cell membrane, about seventy thousandths of a volt, and divide by the thickness of that membrane, about five nanometres. The answer is roughly ten million volts per metre. Dry air breaks down into a spark at around three million. Every cell in your body sits inside a field several times stronger than that, and has done since you were an embryo.

Nothing sparks, because the membrane is a two-molecule-thick sheet of oil, and oil insulates beautifully. A protein called the sodium–potassium pump burns one ATP to throw three sodium ions out and haul two potassium ions in, over and over. The voltage itself appears when potassium leaks back down the gradient the pump built, leaving the inside negative.

That is a charged battery, and a cell spends it like one: to drag glucose uphill against its own concentration, to push waste out, and, in nerve and muscle, to fire. The bill is enormous. A large share of the energy you burn doing nothing at all goes to pumps maintaining a difference across a film you will never see.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why is a resting cell membrane better described as a charged battery than as a wall?
A: Because pumps burn ATP to hold unequal ion concentrations across it, which stores a voltage. The cell then spends that voltage to move molecules uphill and, in nerve and muscle, to fire.
