# The paper that fifteen journals did not want

By her own account it was rejected by about fifteen journals before the *Journal of Theoretical Biology* ran it in 1967. The author, publishing then as Lynn Sagan, claimed that the organelles burning sugar inside every one of your cells are descendants of bacteria that got swallowed and never digested.

Her evidence was circumstantial and strange. Mitochondria carry their own DNA, in a loop, the way bacteria do. They build their own ribosomes, and those ribosomes are bacterial enough that certain antibiotics attack them. They divide by pinching in half on their own schedule; your cell cannot assemble one from parts. They sit inside two membranes, as though the outer one were the leftover wrapping of a meal.

Biologists disliked it, partly because it sounded like a fable about cooperation in a field organised around competition. Then gene sequencing arrived, and mitochondrial genes landed squarely inside one bacterial group, the alphaproteobacteria. You are not one organism that acquired a battery. You are a merger that held.
