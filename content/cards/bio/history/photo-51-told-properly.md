# Photo 51, told properly

On 2 May 1952 Raymond Gosling, working under Rosalind Franklin at King's College London, exposed an X-ray film of a DNA fibre held at high humidity. The result is a blurred X of spots. That X is the signature of a helix, and the spacings give you the pitch and the diameter.

The popular telling turns Franklin into a technician who took a photograph she did not understand. She was a crystallographer, she had already established that the phosphate backbone faces outward, and she had determined the crystal symmetry — which by itself implies the two strands run in opposite directions.

What actually happened is smaller and worse. In January 1953 Maurice Wilkins showed the photograph to James Watson without asking her. Separately, Max Perutz passed Watson and Crick an unpublished Medical Research Council progress report containing her measurements. Neither act was theft in any legal sense. Neither was requested.

Franklin died of ovarian cancer in 1958, aged 37. The Nobel was awarded in 1962, and it is not given posthumously — so the question of whether she would have shared it has no answer, only an argument.
