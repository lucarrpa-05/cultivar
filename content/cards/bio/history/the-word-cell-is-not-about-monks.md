# The word "cell" is not about monks

In January 1665 Robert Hooke published *Micrographia*, sliced cork thin enough to see through, and found it built of tiny empty boxes. He called them cells.

The story you were told — that he named them after monks' sleeping rooms — is not in the book. Hooke's own comparison is a honeycomb, and "cell" was already ordinary anatomical vocabulary for a small chamber. The monastery version is a nineteenth-century embellishment that survives because it is charming and because it flattered cell theory.

What Hooke saw, in any case, were the dead walls of plant cells. Living ones arrived a decade later from Delft, where a draper named Antonie van Leeuwenhoek ground lenses better than anyone in Europe and wrote to the Royal Society in 1676 about little animals swimming in rainwater, and in 1683 about the ones in his own dental scrapings. The Society did not believe him at first. He never revealed his lens-making method, and nobody reproduced it for centuries.

Two men, two decades, and the observation that biology would eventually be rebuilt on: everything alive is made of these, and every one of them comes from another one.
