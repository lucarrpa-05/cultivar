# A jar of gas, a spark, and amino acids by Friday

In 1952 a graduate student named Stanley Miller talked Harold Urey into letting him try something nobody thought would work. He sealed water, methane, ammonia and hydrogen into glass tubing, boiled the water so the vapour circulated, and fired continuous electric sparks through it to stand in for lightning. He left it running.

Within a week the water had gone pink-brown. In it were amino acids — the units proteins are built from — assembled out of nothing but gas and electricity. The paper appeared in *Science* in 1953, the same year as the double helix.

Two honest corrections. Current models say the early atmosphere was probably much less hospitable to that chemistry than Miller assumed: mostly carbon dioxide and nitrogen rather than methane and ammonia. So his particular recipe is probably not Earth's. And a coda in his favour: he kept his sealed vials, and a re-analysis in 2008 with modern instruments found far more amino acids than 1950s methods could detect.

What still nobody has done is get from a soup of parts to something that copies itself. That, not the parts, is the problem.
