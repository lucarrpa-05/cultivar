# Mendel's data are too good to be true

In 1936 Ronald Fisher went back through the pea counts, carrying the statistics he had largely invented himself. His question was new. Not *do these data fit the theory* — everyone agreed they did. Do they fit it **too well**?

Real sampling is noisy. Flip a thousand fair coins and you land near five hundred, not on it. Mendel's ratios hug the predicted values across experiment after experiment, more snugly than chance permits: pooled, the chi-squared statistic came out implausibly *small*, and of one series Fisher wrote that "a deviation as fortunate as Mendel's is to be expected once in twenty-nine trials". His verdict was careful and brutal: the data "have been falsified to agree closely with Mendel's expectations" — possibly, he allowed, by an assistant who knew what answer was wanted.

The argument has run for ninety years. Re-analyses since — Hartl and Fairbanks in 2007, a book-length reassessment in 2008 — conclude there is no good case that Mendel fabricated anything.

What makes this a card about mathematics rather than gossip: you can only detect data that are too good if you have a model of how noisy honest data should be.

Q: What was Fisher's actual argument against Mendel's counts, and what is the current verdict?
A: Not that the ratios were wrong but that they were too close to the predicted values — pooled across his experiments the chi-squared statistic came out implausibly small, which is what an honest sample almost never does. After re-analyses by Hartl and Fairbanks (2007) and a book-length reassessment in 2008, the consensus is that deliberate fabrication is not supported; unconscious bias in classifying ambiguous peas is the most defended reading.
