# He got better at a task he swore he had never done

On 1 September 1953 the surgeon William Scoville removed the medial temporal lobes from both sides of a 27-year-old man’s brain. The seizures improved. The patient became the most studied person in the history of neuroscience: Henry Molaison, H.M. in the literature until he died in 2008.

He could hold a conversation and score well on intelligence tests. He could not form new conscious memories. Brenda Milner worked with him for decades and he met her as a stranger every time.

Then she gave him a mirror-drawing task: trace a star seeing only its reflection, which is unnatural and hard. He made roughly thirty errors on his first attempt and five or six by the tenth. The next day he started better than he had finished — and told her, sincerely, that he had never seen it before.

So memory is not one thing. Episodes and facts run through the hippocampus. Skill does not. You can lose your biography and keep your hands.

Twenty years later Bliss and Lømo found a cellular version: a burst of stimulation leaves a synapse stronger for hours — the potentiation Hebb had guessed at in 1949.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: What did H.M.'s mirror-drawing performance show that his conversation could not?
A: That skill learning and conscious recollection are separate systems. His hands accumulated practice across days while the hippocampal system that would have let him *remember* practising was gone — so he improved at something he sincerely believed he had never done.
