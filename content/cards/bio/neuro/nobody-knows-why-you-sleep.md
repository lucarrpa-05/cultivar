# Nobody knows why you sleep

Every animal anyone has studied closely does something like it, and the cost is outrageous: a third of your life spent immobile, unresponsive, not eating, not watching for anything. Evolution does not usually tolerate a bill that size without a return.

It is also not optional. Rats kept awake die within weeks. Fatal familial insomnia — a prion disease caused by a single letter change in one gene — wrecks the thalamus and kills in about eighteen months, with unstoppable sleeplessness as its leading symptom.

So what is the return? The favourite answer of the last decade was housekeeping. A 2013 paper reported that the space between brain cells expands by around 60% during sleep and that waste proteins wash out about twice as fast. Then in 2024 a group measuring clearance a different way reported the reverse: roughly 30% *less* clearance asleep, 50% less under anaesthesia. Both results are in the literature and the mechanism is unsettled.

What survives: sleep does something for memory and something for metabolism, and you cannot skip it. This is an open problem sitting at the centre of a field rather than at its edge.
