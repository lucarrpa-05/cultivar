# Everything we know about nerves, we learned from a squid

In 1936 J. Z. Young took a second look at a pale tube running down the inside of a squid's mantle. He had assumed it was a blood vessel, because nothing that wide could be a nerve fibre. It was a nerve fibre — a single one, up to a millimetre and a half across, perhaps a thousand times the diameter of the ones in your arm. (L. W. Williams had described them in 1909; the work was forgotten, and Williams died in a lift accident three years later.)

The squid has it for speed. Fat axons conduct faster, and the animal uses this one to fire the escape jet that gets it away from a fish.

Fat also means you can push a wire *inside* it. That is what Hodgkin and Huxley did at Plymouth from 1939: they recorded the voltage across a living membrane from within the cell. Every textbook picture of how a nerve fires comes out of an animal that evolved a giant nerve for a reason that had nothing to do with us.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why was the squid giant axon the breakthrough preparation for electrophysiology?
A: Because it is enormous — up to about 1.5 mm across. That is wide enough to insert an electrode inside a single living axon and clamp or measure the voltage across its membrane directly, which no ordinary neuron allowed at the time.
