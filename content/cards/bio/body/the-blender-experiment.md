# The kitchen blender that proved genes are made of DNA

In 1952 most biologists still expected genes to be protein. Proteins have twenty kinds of building block and DNA has four; four letters looked too dull to write an organism. Avery and colleagues had shown otherwise in 1944; the field shrugged.

Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase made it stick, with a Waring blender. They grew bacteriophages — viruses that hunt bacteria — in two batches. One batch was fed radioactive sulphur, which goes into protein and not into DNA. The other got radioactive phosphorus, which goes into DNA and not into protein. Then they let each batch of phages latch onto *E. coli* and ran the mixture in the blender, which shears off anything still stuck to the outside of a cell.

The sulphur stayed outside. The phosphorus went in, and the infected cells went on to make new phages. Whatever carries the instructions had entered the bacterium, and it was not the protein.

The experiment works because of what a virus is: a protein container with a genome inside and a mechanism for injecting it. The box waits on the doorstep. The instructions go in and take over the kitchen.
