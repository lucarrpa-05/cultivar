# They signed away insulin for a dollar each

In 1921, in a borrowed Toronto laboratory, Frederick Banting and Charles Best pulled something out of dog pancreas that dropped blood sugar. James Collip purified it enough to put into a person. On 11 January 1922 a fourteen-year-old named Leonard Thompson got the first injection; the first batch was too crude and the second worked. Children who had been left in wards to die in diabetic comas woke up.

Banting, Best and Collip assigned the patent to the University of Toronto for one dollar each. The line everyone quotes — that insulin belonged to the world and not to him — is not documented in anything Banting wrote or said; it seems to trace back to a children's biography published in 1959. The patent was also not purely a gift: holding it stopped anyone else from patenting insulin and let Toronto set quality standards for the firms that manufactured it.

What they had found is the cleanest example of a hormone: a molecule built in one organ, poured into the blood, and read by cells across the whole body as a single instruction — take up glucose, now.
