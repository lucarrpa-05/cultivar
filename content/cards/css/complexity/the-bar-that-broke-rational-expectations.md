# The bar on Canyon Road that broke rational expectations

In 1984 three Los Alamos men — George Cowan, Nick Metropolis, Stirling Colgate — plus Murray Gell-Mann and David Pines from outside the lab founded an institute in Santa Fe with no departments, on the theory that the interesting problems were the ones nobody's discipline owned.

W. Brian Arthur, an economist there, kept going to El Farol on Canyon Road for the Irish music. The bar was fun when it was not crowded and unpleasant when more than about 60% of the regulars turned up, and everybody decided independently, every Thursday, without knowing what anyone else would do.

That is an impossible problem for the standard theory. If everyone reasons identically and the shared forecast says "quiet", everyone goes and it is packed; if it says "packed", nobody goes and it is empty. Any deductively rational common expectation destroys itself.

Arthur's answer, in 1994, was that people do not deduce. They carry a handful of rules of thumb, use whichever has been working, and drop it when it stops. The crowd finds its way to roughly 60% and stays there — an equilibrium nobody computed.

## Recall
type: reveal
Q: Why can't the El Farol problem be solved by everyone reasoning correctly?
A: Because a correct shared forecast refutes itself. If the common expectation is "it will be quiet", everyone attends and it is crowded; if it is "crowded", nobody attends. Any symmetric deductive rule is self-defeating, which is why Arthur models people as switching between rules of thumb instead.
