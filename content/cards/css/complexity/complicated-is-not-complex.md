# A jet engine is complicated. A city is complex.

A Boeing 747 has millions of parts and is *complicated*: every part has a specification, the interactions are designed, and with the pieces and the blueprint you understand the plane. Take one out and you can say what breaks.

A city has fewer kinds of part and nobody understands it. Remove a bus route and the consequences arrive in housing prices six years later. That is *complex*, and the difference is not size. It is that the parts adapt to each other.

Warren Weaver, writing in *American Scientist* in 1948, split science into three eras. Until about 1900 it handled **problems of simplicity**: two or three variables, differential equations, planets and projectiles. Then statistics conquered **disorganized complexity**: variables so numerous and so independent that averages do everything, as in a gas.

Between those sits the gap Weaver named: **organized complexity**. A moderate number of parts, strongly interacting, with structure that neither collapses into a few equations nor washes out into an average. Economies. Ecosystems. Immune systems. Traffic.

Weaver's bet was that computers and mixed teams would eventually crack that middle. Agent-based modelling is one of the things that turned up.
