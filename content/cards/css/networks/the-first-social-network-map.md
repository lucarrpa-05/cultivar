# The runaway epidemic that was solved by drawing dots

The New York State Training School for Girls in Hudson had a problem that looked like contagion: girls kept absconding, in waves, and nothing about them individually predicted it. Not age, not offence, not how long they had been there.

Jacob Moreno did something nobody had thought to do with a social problem. He asked every girl who she wanted to sit with, live with, work with — and then he *drew it*. Dots for girls, arrows for choices. He called the picture a sociogram and published it in *Who Shall Survive?* in 1934.

The map explained the waves. Runaways were not distributed at random through the school; they travelled along the arrows. A girl's position in the friendship structure predicted her behaviour better than anything about the girl.

That is the founding claim of the whole field, and it is easy to forget how strange it was in 1934: that a property of your *relationships* can be a cause, on a par with a property of you. Every network measure since is a way of making that idea precise.

Q: What was Moreno's founding move, in one sentence?
A: He treated the pattern of relationships as the explanatory variable — the runaways were predicted by who a girl was connected to, not by anything measured about the girl herself.
