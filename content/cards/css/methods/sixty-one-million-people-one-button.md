# Sixty-one million people, one button, one day

2 November 2010, US congressional elections. Every American Facebook user over 18 who logged in that day — about 61 million people — was randomly assigned to one of three conditions. Some saw nothing. Some saw a banner with a polling-place link and an "I Voted" button. Some saw the same banner plus the faces of up to six friends who had already clicked it.

The faces were the treatment. Bond and colleagues then matched users to public voting records, so the outcome was not a click or a self-report but whether a real person actually turned out.

The direct effect was small and real: roughly 60,000 extra votes from people who saw the social message. The indirect effect was larger — about 280,000 more from their friends, who never saw a banner at all. Around 340,000 additional votes in total, and close friends — 7% of Facebook friendships — accounted for essentially all of the contagion.

Two things happened at once here. Social science got causal identification at a scale nobody had ever had. And a company demonstrated, in public, that it could move an election's turnout with a design decision.
